Emergency

Channel 16 · High power · Speak slowly

UKCoast: who to call

HM Coastguard on ch 16 and DSC ch 70. Routine calls in the Solent: ch 67.

No radio? Phone 999 or 112 and ask for the Coastguard.

UKInland

The handbook covers UK coastal use only. On inland waters, phone 999 / 112.

NLCoast and sea: who to call

Kustwacht (JRCC Den Helder): permanent watch on ch 16 and DSC ch 70. Voice name "Netherlands Coastguard"; during a rescue "Den Helder Rescue". MMSI 002442000.

Phone: 0900-0111 (in nood) · +31 88 958 4000 · 112

0900 numbers only work from a Dutch phone inside the Netherlands. With a foreign phone, or from abroad, use +31 88 958 4000 or 112.

Not urgent (engine, fouled prop): KNRM Helpt +31 88 999 61 00 or the KNRM Helpt app.

NLInland waters: who to call

Rivers and canals: MAYDAY by voice on ch 10, or on the blokkanaal (traffic post) of the area you're in.

IJsselmeer, Markermeer, Waddenzee, Ooster- and Westerschelde: the Kustwacht keeps watch on ch 16; use ch 16 for distress there.

No DSC on inland waters (not permitted, except voluntarily on certain large waters). Power is limited to 1 W, so also phone: 0900-0111 · 112.

NOCoast: who to call

Kystradio Sør and Kystradio Nord, run by the Joint Rescue Coordination Centre (Hovedredningssentralen) since 1 Jan 2026. Watch on ch 16 around the clock and receive DSC alerts.

Phone: 120 (Kystradio) · 112 police · 113 medical · 110 fire

Kystradio Sør +47 51 69 00 44 · Kystradio Nord +47 75 52 89 25

With a foreign (e.g. Dutch) phone: save the numbers with +47. 112 always works. 113 and 110 usually work but are not guaranteed. Service numbers 120, 02016 and 02800 probably don't work from a foreign SIM. For non-urgent help (engine, fouled prop) call Kystradio on its +47 number or use the RS SafeTrx app.

Not urgent: Redningsselskapet assistance 02016.

NOKyst: hvem du kontakter

Kystradio Sør og Kystradio Nord, drevet av Hovedredningssentralen siden 1. januar 2026. Lyttevakt på kanal 16 hele døgnet, og de mottar DSC-nødalarmer.

Telefon: 120 (Kystradio) · 112 politi · 113 medisinsk nødhjelp · 110 brann

Kystradio Sør +47 51 69 00 44 · Kystradio Nord +47 75 52 89 25

Med utenlandsk (f.eks. nederlandsk) telefon: lagre numrene med +47. 112 virker alltid. 113 og 110 virker som regel, men ikke garantert. Tjenestenumrene 120, 02016 og 02800 virker trolig ikke med utenlandsk SIM. Ved behov for hjelp som ikke haster (motor, noe i propellen): ring Kystradio på +47-nummeret eller bruk appen RS SafeTrx.

Ikke akutt: Redningsselskapets assistanse 02016.

NOLakes and inland waters

DSC DISTRESS ALERT

  1. Lift the cover of the red Distress button.
  2. If there's time: press and release it (or use the Call menu) and pick the nature of distress: Fire/explosion, Flooding, Collision, Grounding, Listing, Sinking, Disabled and adrift, Abandoning ship, Piracy, Man overboard. Otherwise it sends "Undesignated".
  3. Press and hold Distress for about 5 seconds until it transmits. Your MMSI and GPS position are included.
  4. The alert repeats automatically about every 4 minutes until acknowledged or cancelled.
  5. Radio switches to ch 16. Immediately give the voice Mayday with your MMSI.
Sent it by accident? Use False alert sent.

I HEAR A MAYDAY

  1. Write it down: name, callsign/MMSI, position, problem, people on board.
  2. Keep radio silence on that channel unless you are acknowledging and helping.
  3. A coast station has probably heard it: give them 5 minutes to respond.
  4. Can't help effectively? Don't acknowledge.
  5. No response and you can help: acknowledge by voice, then report your position, speed and ETA.
  6. No acknowledgement at all and no sign of contact with rescuers: send a Mayday Relay.
  7. DSC alert received: same rules. Class D can't acknowledge or relay by DSC; if not acknowledged within 5 min, call the casualty and the Coastguard by voice on 16.

FALSE ALERT

First rule: don't repeat it.
  1. Switch the DSC controller off (usually the radio off) to stop automatic repeats. If your set has a dedicated cancel distress function, use that instead.
  2. Switch back on, select ch 16 and high power.
  3. Send this cancel message:

All stations, All stations, All stations

this is [boat name], [boat name], [boat name]

[callsign, spelled phonetically]

MMSI [MMSI], I say again, MMSI [MMSI]

Cancel my distress alert, I say again, cancel my distress alert

OUT

Voice procedure

Routine calls

Switch on and set up

Example: calling a marina a couple of miles away on channel 80.

  1. Switch the power supply to the radio on
  2. Adjust the dimmer if necessary, to illuminate the display and controls
  3. Turn the squelch right down, to produce a frying sound from the loudspeaker
  4. Adjust the volume until the frying sound is loud but tolerable
  5. Adjust the squelch until the frying sound just stops
  6. Select the channel (here: 80)
  7. Select low transmitter power
  8. LISTEN, to make sure the channel is not in use
  9. Press the PTT to speak
  10. Release the PTT to listen for a reply

Golden rules on air

  • Listen before you transmit.
  • Think before you speak: plan what you will say.
  • Hold the mic 2–5 cm from your mouth, slightly to one side.
  • Speak normally or slightly louder. Don't shout.
  • Press to talk, release to listen.
  • End with Over (reply expected) or Out (no reply). Never "over and out".
  • Identify yourself every time you press the PTT. Never repeat names more than three times.
  • Calls on ch 16 kept to a minimum; apart from distress and safety, never longer than one minute.

Calling another vessel

Call on ch 16 (unless another channel was agreed), then move to an inter-ship working channel (6, 8, 72, 77). Use low power if possible. The calling vessel proposes the working channel. The whole ch 16 exchange should take less than a minute.

Karenita, Karenita, this is Blue Tango, Blue Tango, Over.

Blue Tango, this is Karenita, Over.

Karenita, this is Blue Tango, Channel Seventy Two, please, Seven Two, Over.

Blue Tango, this is Karenita, Channel Seventy Two, Over.

Both switch to ch 72. Whoever said "over" last is waiting, so the other station calls:

Karenita, Karenita, this is Blue Tango, Blue Tango, Over.

Blue Tango, this is Karenita, Over.

Karenita, this is Blue Tango. We're heading for Helford this evening, and wondered if you'd like to meet up with us there. Over.

Blue Tango, this is Karenita. This sounds good to me. Shall we see you at eight, then? Over.

Karenita, this is Blue Tango. Yes, we will see you there at eight. Out.

Blue Tango, this is Karenita. Out.

Both vessels switch back to channel 16.

Calling a coast station (port, marina)

Call directly on the station's working channel, not on ch 16. Coast stations pay per channel, so ch 16 may not be among them. Find channels in the Admiralty List of Radio Signals, Admiralty Maritime Communications or a nautical almanac. Say which channel you are on and describe your vessel briefly.

Dover Port Control, Dover Port Control, this is Motor Yacht Galadriel, Galadriel, on Channel 74, Over.

Galadriel, Dover Port Control, Over.

Dover Port Control, this is Galadriel. I am the 15 metre motor cruiser about a mile south of your western entrance. I am about two minutes away and would like permission to enter. Over.

Galadriel, Dover Port Control. We have a fast ferry about to leave. Do not enter port now, but wait to the south of the entrance, and stand by on this channel until we call you in. Out.

Dover Port Control, this is Galadriel. Standing by. Out.

Calling the Coastguard

Call on ch 16, as if calling a vessel. The Coastguard chooses the working channel (usually 67). Exception: Solent Coastguard is called on ch 67 for routine matters. UK

NLCall "Netherlands Coastguard" on ch 16 (or DSC). Weather, gale warnings and safety messages: announced on ch 16 / 70, broadcast on ch 23 and 83.

NLInland you normally call the traffic post on the blokkanaal, or a lock or bridge on the channel shown on its sign. Calling: name up to 3×, "hier is" / "this is", own name, "over"; end with "uit" / "out".

NOCall Kystradio Sør or Kystradio Nord on ch 16 (or a routine DSC call); they move you to a working channel. Weather and navigational warnings every 3 hours; you can ask for the latest forecast.

Radio check

Only when needed (e.g. after fitting a new antenna). With any station willing to co-operate, on a channel they listen to: a marina on its working channel, a friend on an agreed channel, or the Coastguard on ch 16 (ch 67 in the Solent).

Cardhu, Cardhu, this is Windrift, Windrift. Radio check, please. Over.

Windrift, this is Cardhu. You are loud and clear. Out.

Garbled and unanswered calls

Clearly for you, but you missed who called: reply with "Station calling Karenita, this is Karenita, Over."

Not sure it was for you: don't answer. The caller will repeat within a few minutes.

No answer to your call: wait at least 2 minutes between attempts, try 3 times, then you may start again 3 minutes later. Meanwhile check: antenna cable, battery voltage, dual watch off, volume up.

Link calls (public correspondence)

A coast radio station connects your VHF call to the shore telephone network. Closed in the UK, Ireland, France and the Netherlands; still available in e.g. Belgium, Channel Islands, Denmark, Germany, Spain, Portugal.

  • Sign up with a Maritime Accounting Authority (MAA) first; its AAIC (Accounting Authority Identity Code, 4 characters) goes on your Ship Radio Licence application.
  • Call on the station's working channel. Listen first: only go ahead if you hear silence (not an operator, one side of a phone call, phone noises or regular beeps).
  • You may hear beeps confirming your call is queued. When finished, call the station again on the same channel and end with "Thank you. Out."
  • Shore-to-ship: the station broadcasts a traffic list at set times. If you hear your name, call them and say "you have traffic for me".

Hiring a boat (boot huren)

Which name and identity you give depends on the boat and on which radio you use. The rule: the name is the boat's (so rescuers know what to look for); the radio identity (MMSI, call sign) belongs to the transmitter you are using.

SituationSay as nameIdentity (call sign / MMSI)
Boat has a fixed VHF: use it (more power, higher antenna)Boat's nameThe boat's call sign and MMSI
Boat has a name, you use your handheldBoat's nameYour handheld's MMSI (and your own call sign if RDI gave you one). Not the boat's call sign
Boat has no name and no VHF, you use your handheldA short description, e.g. "white rental motorboat"; add hull or registration number, or "rental boat from [company]"Your handheld's MMSI (and own call sign)

A DSC alert from your handheld always sends your handheld's MMSI plus its GPS position, whatever boat you are on.

Before you cast off

  • Ask the hire company for the boat's name, call sign and MMSI, and whether the fixed VHF has DSC and a GPS connected.
  • No name? Take a photo of the boat and note colour, length, type and any number on the hull.
  • Enter it under Emergency → My boat (description and persons on board), so the scripts are ready.
  • Check your handheld: charged, on the right channel group, ATIS code programmed for Dutch inland waters.
  • Save the emergency numbers with country code in your phone (see the country rules below).

NLNetherlands: local rules

Licence and certificate

  • Register every marifoon (fixed and handheld) with RDI. You get a callsign, an ATIS code and (if needed) an MMSI; you must program them correctly. No registration = no maritime transmitter on board.
  • Exams (theory at the CBR, practical at CBR-recognised trainers) and certificate applications go via the CBR; radio registration via RDI.
  • Basiscertificaat Marifonie: inland waters only. Marcom-B: inland and coastal waters; needed for sea mode with DSC. Marcom-A: inland, coast and sea. A foreign certificate such as an SRC can be used for registration.
  • Fixed marifoon compulsory on vessels of 20 m or more. Up to 20 m a handheld is allowed on inland waters.
  • Dutch MMSIs start with 244, 245, 246.

Inland waters (rivers, canals, lakes; also IJsselmeer and Wadden for listening)

  • Listen on ch 10 (ship to ship, calling, safety). Fallback channel: 13.
  • Blokgebied: a VHF sign with a red border means you must listen on that blokkanaal; a blue sign is an information channel (optional). Go back to ch 10 when you leave.
  • Bridges and locks: use the channel on the sign, then return to 10 or the blokkanaal.
  • Private conversations only on 72 and 77. On-board handhelds: 15 and 17.
  • ATIS is mandatory: your code goes out automatically when you release the PTT.
  • DSC is not permitted in inland navigation, except voluntarily on certain large Dutch waters. A DSC alert would hardly be received anyway (traffic posts and most inland ships have no DSC).
  • Max transmitter power 1 W; the set reduces power automatically on ship-to-ship channels.
  • Dual watch is not allowed on inland waters.
  • IJsselmeer: ch 1 is the information channel of the Centrale Meldpost (broadcasts about 15 min past the hour). Don't stay on it; go back to 10.
  • Distress, urgency and safety use the same prowords (MAYDAY, PAN PAN, SECURITE), on ch 10 / the blokkanaal; on IJsselmeer, Markermeer, Wadden and Zeeland waters on ch 16. Non-participating ships keep radio silence.
  • Small vessels with a marifoon on board must listen out (BPR art. 4.05). "Klein schip: aanmelden mag, antwoorden verplicht": reporting in is optional, answering when called is compulsory.
  • Blue square sign = informatiekanaal (listening recommended; only ask for information such as berths, harbour dues, bridge times). Square sign with red border = navigatiekanaal (usually ch 10 or a VTS sector channel): listening compulsory.
  • Common channels: 18, 20, 22 bridges and locks · 31 often used by marinas · 77 social. Check blokkanalen for your route beforehand at vaarweginformatie.nl or in the Wateralmanak deel 1.
  • Calling: press the PTT, wait about 1 second, then speak. Use the 4 W's: who you are, what you are, where you are, where you're going / what you want.
  • Position words: afvaart (downstream, towards the sea) / opvaart (upstream); boven / beneden a lock or bridge (boven = higher water); ter hoogte van a landmark; left/right bank; kilometre post.
  • Rijkswaterstaat: 0800-8002.

Coast and sea

  • Listen on ch 16. Power up to 25 W; DSC allowed (needs Marcom-B or higher).
  • Kustwacht watches ch 16, DSC ch 70 and MF DSC 2187.5 kHz. Voice name "Netherlands Coastguard", in SAR "Den Helder Rescue", MMSI 002442000.
  • Gale warnings, forecasts and safety messages: announced on 16/70, broadcast on ch 23 and 83, and Navtex 518 kHz.
  • Medical advice (Radio Medische Dienst): call the Kustwacht on ch 16; you are connected to a doctor on a working channel.
  • Phone: 0900-0111 · +31 88 958 4000 · 112. Not urgent: KNRM Helpt +31 88 999 61 00.

Key facts to memorise

  1. Distress = a vessel, vehicle, aircraft or person in grave and imminent danger.
  2. Distress message order: MIPDANIO — Mayday, Identity, Position, Distress, Assistance, Number, Information, Over. Exam marks are often lost by forgetting the Mayday and Identity parts.
  3. Mayday call: Mayday ×3, this is, name ×3, callsign (and MMSI). Not addressed to anyone, no "over".
  4. Ch 16 = distress, safety, calling. Ch 70 = DSC only. Ch 75/76 = guard band, max 1 W.
  5. Max power 25 W; every set must have a low-power setting of 1 W or less. Handhelds typically 3–6 W.
  6. VHF horizon ≈ 3 × √(height in m) nautical miles. Range = sum of both horizons.
  7. Marine VHF ≈ 156 MHz, wavelength ≈ 1.92 m, FM. Optimum antenna ≈ 93 cm.
  8. Unanswered call: wait ≥ 2 min, max 3 tries, restart after 3 min.
  9. Hearing a Mayday: allow the Coastguard 5 minutes; only acknowledge if you can help; otherwise keep silence and write it down.
  10. DSC distress button: covered, hold ~5 s. Alert repeats about every 4 min until acknowledged/cancelled. Always follow with voice Mayday on ch 16.
  11. Class D cannot acknowledge or relay DSC distress alerts. Don't use Class A/B to do so either, unless SAR authorities instruct.
  12. DSC urgency/safety announcement carries only your MMSI and the working channel: always follow up by voice.
  13. False DSC alert: switch off controller, back on, ch 16, high power, "All stations ×3… cancel my distress alert… Out".
  14. MMSI: ship MID+6; coast 00+MID+4; group 0+MID+5. UK MIDs 232–235.
  15. EPIRB 406 MHz + homing 121.5 MHz + light. Hex ID 15 chars. Battery ~5 yrs, hydrostatic release every 2 yrs, test each season, never send an alert while testing.
  16. Radar-SART: 12 dots, 0.6 M apart, become arcs within ~1 M, circles when close. AIS-SART: red cross in a circle. Mount ≥ 1 m; not with a radar reflector.
  17. Navtex 518 kHz (international, English) and 490 kHz (national). Leave it switched on.
  18. GMDSS sea areas: A1 VHF, A2 MF, A3 Inmarsat, A4 polar (beyond Inmarsat).
  19. Ship Radio Licence = the vessel (like car tax). Authority to Operate (SRC/LRC) = the person (like a driving licence).
  20. Secrecy: don't eavesdrop, don't gossip.
1

Introduction

Types of message

  • Distress, Urgency and Safety
  • Routine: between vessels; with harbour authorities (port operations); with traffic monitoring/control authorities
  • Public correspondence ("link calls"), paid
  • On-board communications

Types of user

Anything afloat with a radio is a Ship Radio Station, from cruise liner to canoe. Shore stations (Coastguard, port, marina) are Coast Radio Stations, licensed separately and more restrictively. Aircraft such as Coastguard helicopters also carry marine VHF.

Types of equipment

Fixed: mounted, powered from the boat's electrics, separate antenna. Portable: own rechargeable battery and stubby antenna.

2

How radio works

Frequency and wavelength

1 Hz = 1 cycle per second; kHz = thousand, MHz = million, GHz = billion. Radio waves travel at 300,000,000 m/s.

Wavelength = 300,000,000 / Frequency. Marine VHF ≈ 156 MHz, so wavelength ≈ 300/156 ≈ 1.92 m.

Bands

  • LF/VLF: diffract easily around obstacles and the horizon; long-range broadcast and navigation.
  • MF: medium range, regional broadcast, beacons.
  • HF: bounced back by the ionosphere; world-wide communication.
  • VHF/UHF: line of sight, not bent over hills or the horizon; local use. Range depends on antenna heights.

VHF horizon (miles) ≈ 3 × √(antenna height in m). Motor cruiser at 4 m → 6 M; yacht at 16 m → 12 M; together ≈ 18 M. Full table under Reference → VHF range.

Modulation

AM varies the wave's amplitude (MF broadcasting; SSB in marine MF). FM varies the frequency; technically superior, used for VHF.

Antennas

A vertical antenna works best slightly shorter than half a wavelength: about 93 cm for marine VHF. Power comes out of the sides, not the ends. Every 3 dB doubles the signal.

  • 3 dBi: tolerant of heel and pitch; recommended for sailing yachts.
  • 6 dBi: twice as focused, suffers when tilted; motorboats, if mounted upright.
  • 1.5 dBi: least focused, fine at an angle; portables.

Use proper marine feeder cable, not domestic TV cable. Have an antenna installed professionally where possible.

Batteries

  • Alkaline: not rechargeable; good reserve for some portables. 1.5 V/cell.
  • Lithium: not rechargeable, long shelf life; EPIRBs, emergency radios.
  • Lead-acid: heavy, rechargeable, economical. Heavy duty (engine starting, avoid >30% discharge), deep cycle/traction (domestic), leisure (compromise). Gel/AGM versions need their own chargers.
  • NiCad: thousands of cycles, but self-discharge and "memory effect"; lower voltage than alkaline.
  • NiMH: more capacity than NiCad, little memory effect, self-discharges.
  • Lithium-ion: most energy per weight, expensive, sensitive to overcharging.

Recharge NiCads only when flat. Top up other rechargeables whenever you can. Don't overcharge any rechargeable, especially Li-ion. Never recharge alkaline or lithium.

Rules and regulations

Who makes the rules

ITU (International Telecommunication Union) holds a World Radio Conference every 2–3 years; result: the Radio Regulations, an international treaty. IMO regulates shipping generally (COLREGs, traffic separation, SOLAS). In the UK: Ofcom (technical, licensing) and the MCA (equipment, qualifications). UK penalties: up to £5000, six months' prison, confiscation. UK

Two licences needed

Ship Radio Licence: for the vessel, like taxing a car. UK: free via Ofcom, valid up to 10 years unless owner, address or equipment changes; carry on board; covers all radios incl. the tender's portable. Gives a callsign (4–5 letters/numbers) and optionally an MMSI. UK

Ship Portable Radio Licence: for a person; one portable VHF (plus a PLB/EPIRB) on multiple vessels, UK waters only; gives a "T" number instead of a callsign. UK

Coast Station Licences: not free; types for private UK channels, international, marina (channels M, M2, 80), training schools, suppliers.

Authority to Operate: for the operator, like a driving licence, for life. Voluntary-fit vessels: SRC (VHF) or LRC (VHF, MF, HF, satellite).

Type approval

Equipment must conform to the Radio Equipment Directive (RED), with a declaration of conformity and the CE mark; SOLAS vessels need MED approval (ship's wheel mark). Many US-market radios are illegal to install under a UK licence.

Radio log

Regulations require a record of distress, urgency and safety messages and important events. UK pleasure craft are exempt, but noting them in the deck log is good practice.

Secrecy

It is an offence to intercept communications not meant for the public, or to divulge what you heard. Don't eavesdrop. Don't gossip.

The ten commandments

  1. Do not transmit without the authority of the master
  2. Do not transmit false or deceptive distress or safety signals
  3. Do not transmit without identification
  4. Do not shut down before finishing all operations from a distress, urgency or safety call
  5. Do not broadcast, other than distress messages
  6. Do not transmit music
  7. Do not make unnecessary transmissions
  8. Do not use profane, indecent or obscene language
  9. Do not use unauthorised frequencies
  10. Do not transmit messages for shore except via a licensed coast station

Broadcasting = transmitting without saying who it is for and without expecting a reply. Test for "necessary": does it relate to the ship's business?

Often a press-button; on portables often combined with volume.
Volume
Loudspeaker level. Some handsets mute the speaker when lifted.
Squelch
Receiver sensitivity, not volume. Turn up until the frying noise just stops; further risks losing weak signals.

Dimmer

5

VHF channels

Originally 28 channels 50 kHz apart (1–28); channels 60–88 were interleaved later. Every channel has a frequency (or two) and a designated purpose: two boats talking on port-ops ch 12 is possible, but disruptive, dangerous and illegal.

Simplex, duplex, semi-duplex

  • Simplex: one frequency, take turns (single-track road).
  • Duplex: two frequencies, both talk at once (two-lane road). Used for link calls and some port ops.
  • Semi-duplex: duplex channel on a set with one antenna; works like simplex. Ch 80: coast transmits 161.625, ship 157.025, so ships can't talk to each other on it.

Private channels

157.450–160.600 MHz left to national governments. UK: ch 0 (156.000) SAR only; M (157.850) and M2 (161.425) for marinas, clubs and pleasure craft, UK waters only, no certificate needed. M may display as 37 or P1. UK

US channels

Many international duplex channels are simplex in the US system. Calling a UK marina on US ch 80: they hear you, you won't hear them. Check your radio's setting.

Ch 16, guard band and ch 70

Ch 16 = Distress, Safety and Calling. Ch 75 and 76 form the guard band; now allowed for port ops and on-board use at max 1 W. Calls on 16: minimal, never over a minute except distress/safety. Ch 70 is digital (DSC) only, never voice.

NLInland: listen on ch 10 or the blokkanaal; 72/77 for private talk; 15/17 on board; 1 W; ATIS mandatory; no DSC; no dual watch.

NLCoast: listen on 16; Kustwacht safety broadcasts on 23 and 83.

NOListen on 16. VTS centres use 18, 19, 71 or 80 depending on the area (Reference → Norway).

NOLyttevakt på kanal 16. Sjøtrafikksentralene (VTS) bruker kanal 18, 19, 71 eller 80, avhengig av område (Reference → Norge).

Voice procedure 1: routine calls

Plain English with natural rhythm beats half-remembered jargon. Full scripts are on the Routine tab: calling a vessel, a coast station, the Coastguard, radio check, garbled and unanswered calls, link calls. Prowords and the phonetic alphabet are under Reference.

Station called is formally "in control", but best practice now is that the vessel that initiated the call proposes the working channel. With a coast station or the Coastguard, they choose it.

7

Voice procedure 2: distress

A distress call must be authorised by the person in charge of the vessel at the time.

Is it distress? Examples from the book

  • Dog overboard: not a vessel/vehicle/aircraft/person → not distress.
  • Skipper overboard: unless 100% sure of recovery → distress.
  • Dismasted, rig round prop, blown onto rocks 1–2 miles off → distress.
  • Motor boat fouled, drifting 30 miles offshore → not imminent → not distress (Pan-pan).
  • Aground on mud in a sheltered estuary, falling tide → not grave → not distress.
  • Skipper with severe chest pain → judgement of whoever is now in charge.

Call and message

The distress call is the only allowed broadcast. Any channel is legal, but use ch 16, high power. The call is the shout for help; the message (MIPDANIO) gives rescuers the information. Every subsequent message about the incident starts with "Mayday".

Specifying position

  • Lat/long from GPS: check it is valid (just switched on? antenna damaged?). Low battery: transmitting may switch the GPS off.
  • Or bearing and distance FROM a charted landmark, bearings in three figures, degrees true, miles: "one six five degrees from Black Rock Buoy, two decimal four miles". Think: a helicopter flies from the landmark towards you.

Receiving, acknowledging, silence, relay

See the Emergency tab: "I hear a Mayday", "Seelonce" and "Mayday Relay".

8

Voice procedure 3: urgency and safety

Pan-pan: very urgent message about the safety of a ship, aircraft, vehicle or person. Priority over everything except distress. Not a broadcast: addressed to someone, often "All stations". Ch 16, high power, message follows immediately (PDANIO is a good template).

Medical advice: Pan-pan addressed to the Coastguard, "request medical advice". UK: you'll be moved to a duplex channel (62, 63, 64) and linked to a doctor.

Sécurité: gale and navigational warnings, mostly from coast stations. Announced on 16, message on a working channel. A vessel spotting a hazard (e.g. a container) could send one, but telling the Coastguard is usually more useful.

GMDSS

Global Maritime Distress and Safety System: decided by IMO 1979, phased in 1992–1999. Ships carry at least two independent means of alerting SAR ashore, plus ship-to-ship and safety-information reception.

Sea areas

A1
Within VHF coverage of a DSC coast station
A2
Within MF coverage
A3
Within Inmarsat coverage
A4
Beyond Inmarsat (within ~1200 miles of the poles)

Navtex

Written navigational warnings and weather forecasts. 518 kHz: international, always English. 490 kHz: national/local. Each transmitter has a 20-minute slot every 4 hours. Program it to ignore unwanted stations/types; it skips repeats. Leave it on, or it forgets what it has received.

EPIRBs (406 MHz)

  • Sends a coded distress alert with the vessel's identity, detected by Cospas-Sarsat satellites; 15-character Hex ID; may include GNSS position.
  • Also a 121.5 MHz homing signal and a bright flashing light.
  • Cospas-Sarsat (1979; USSR, USA, Canada, France): ≥ 4 low polar-orbit satellites under 1000 km, ~14 orbits/day; position by Doppler. May only download when over a ground station.
  • Geosar: 3 geostationary satellites at ~36,000 km; immediate relay, but need position in the signal.
  • MEOSAR: payloads on GPS, Galileo, Glonass satellites; combines both advantages.
  • Category 1: float-free, auto-activated at 2–4 m depth; stowed in bracket on upper deck. Category 2: manual.
  • Test each season per manufacturer; battery usually every 5 years; hydrostatic release serviced/replaced every 2 years. Never send an alert while testing.
  • Register it (UK Beacon Registry); include on the Ship Radio Licence.
  • Avoid false alarms: switch off when not in use, wrap in two layers of kitchen foil for transport. If triggered by accident: switch off, tell the Coastguard by any means.
  • PLB: personal, 406 MHz, may have 121.5 homing.

SARTs

  • Radar-SART (transponder): answers X-band radar with 12 pulses: a line of 12 dots, 0.6 M apart, outwards from the SART; within ~1 M they become arcs, then 12 concentric circles.
  • AIS-SART (transmitter): GNSS position on AIS; red cross in a circle on the plotter; 9-digit ID.
  • Need radar or AIS on the searching vessel; line of sight. ~5 M normally, under 2 M lying on a liferaft floor, up to 40 M (aircraft). Mount ≥ 1 m above sea; don't use with a radar reflector.

DSC: introduction and routine calls

Digital Selective Calling automates ch 16 procedures. Since April 2000 all radios sold have DSC built in or connectable. A DSC message (format specifier, called MMSI, own MMSI, suggested working channel) lasts ~0.5 s on ch 70. Only the called station's controller alarms; accepting it sends an acknowledgement and switches both radios to the working channel.

Controller classes

A
All facilities incl. HF/MF control, acknowledge/relay distress, geographical area calls. Commercial.
B
Reduced; minimum for vessels required to carry MF and VHF. No HF, no area calls.
D
Small craft, VHF only, reduced capability.
E
Small craft, MF/HF only.

Call types and priorities

Types: Distress, All ships, Individual, Group. Priorities: Distress (Mayday), Urgency (Pan-pan), Safety (Sécurité), Ship's business (not on Class D), Routine.

Position

Connect a GPS so the controller always knows the position (updates about every second). Position and time can be entered manually as back-up.

Sending a routine call (example: Icom DS100)

  1. Press <Call>
  2. Select call type (Individual; Group; All ships; Distress), <Enter>
  3. Enter the MMSI manually or pick from the phone book, <Enter>
  4. Select a working channel, <Enter>
  5. Press <Call> and <Enter> together to transmit

Other makes differ: on a Simrad DSC 1400, <Enter> sends the whole call immediately. Read the manual.

Receiving

Individual: phone-like alarm; one key accepts, acknowledges and switches channel. Group: accepting only mutes the alarm and switches channel; no acknowledgement is sent.

On a Class A or B set, do not use DSC to relay or acknowledge distress messages: use voice.

11

DSC distress, urgency and safety

Same definition of distress. The distress button composes an alert with MMSI and position, sent to all stations on ch 70. Covered button, held ~5 s.

Nature of distress options

Undesignated (default), Fire or explosion, Flooding, Collision, Grounding, Listing / in danger of capsizing, Sinking, Disabled and adrift, Abandoning ship, Piracy, Man overboard.

Read the manual. Practise composing distress alerts. Never send one unless you are really in distress.

After sending

The alert repeats at random intervals of ~4 min until acknowledged or cancelled. It holds less information than a voice Mayday and non-DSC vessels can't receive it, so always follow with a voice Mayday on 16, including your MMSI.

Receiving a DSC distress alert

  • Allow a coast station up to 5 minutes.
  • If you can't help effectively, don't respond.
  • If you can help and hear nothing within 5 min: acknowledge by voice on 16.
  • Try to relay to a coast station by any means.
  • Otherwise keep radio silence.

Class D can't acknowledge or relay DSC alerts; Class A/B shouldn't unless SAR authorities ask. If an alert is not acknowledged within 5 min, contact the casualty by voice on 16 and inform the Coastguard by voice on 16. You may still send a voice Mayday Relay.

Urgency and safety announcements

  1. <Call>
  2. Select "All ships", <Enter>
  3. Select "Urgency" or "Safety", <Enter>
  4. Select working channel (usually 16), <Enter>
  5. <Call> + <Enter> together
  6. Follow up with the voice Pan-pan or Sécurité

Receiving one: accepting mutes the alarm and switches channel; no acknowledgement is sent.
